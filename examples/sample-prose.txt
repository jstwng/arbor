How LLMs learn to be useful.

Pretraining is the first phase. The model is fed enormous corpora of web text, books, code, and conversations. Its only job is to predict the next token. This is brute-force statistical learning: nothing about the data tells the model what is "good" -- it learns the surface texture of human writing. After pretraining, the model can complete sentences fluently but does not yet follow instructions, refuse harm, or stay on task.

Fine-tuning narrows the model's behavior. Engineers collect curated examples of desired conversations -- often thousands or millions of them -- and run supervised training on those. The model learns to mimic the tone, format, and structure of the examples. This is where it starts to feel like a chatbot rather than an autocomplete.

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is the third major phase. Humans rank multiple model responses to the same prompt; a reward model is trained to predict those rankings; the language model is then optimized to maximize the reward. RLHF is what makes the model helpful, honest, and harmless in practice. It also introduces failure modes -- sycophancy, mode collapse, and over-refusal -- that researchers continue to debug.

Inference is what the user sees. The model's weights are frozen, and at runtime tokens are sampled one at a time from the model's predicted distribution. Sampling temperature, top-p, and other decoding parameters control creativity and randomness. The system prompt steers behavior. Tool use, retrieval, and multi-step agents all sit on top of inference -- the underlying model never changes during a conversation.
